Computer players

How the computer players think, value goods, bid, claim land and build; how Claude can sit in a seat and decide in character; and how the simulator plays thousands of games to check the balance.

How a computer player is built In the game

A computer player is a seat at the table like yours. It sees only what a person in that seat would see (its own goods and money, the public board, the plots it has analysed) and it plays only the moves a phone can send. It never reads another player's stock, the game's dice or ground it has not assayed. Every move it makes is checked by the same rules as yours.

Inside, it has layers. The thinking happens once per decision; the hands then carry it out in real time, tick by tick, at a person's speed.

LayerWhat it doesWhen it runs
StrategistLists every option for the decision (which plan for land, which MULE on which plot, buy or sell how much), scores each with named reasons, throws out the ones a hard rule forbids, and hands the best few to the decider.At each decision
Value modelSays what each unit of each good, each plot and each MULE is worth to this seat, in credits.Inside the strategist
OutlookPlays the rest of the month forward on a private copy of what the seat can see, to forecast next month's prices.Inside the value model
Utility scoringTurns each option's reasons into one score from 0 to 1, shaped by the seat's persona.Inside the strategist
DeciderPicks one option from the shortlist and says a line at the table. The built-in decider (Classic) picks by weighted chance among the near-best; Claude can pick instead (Smart).At each decision
HandsCarry the pick out: press Claim when a good plot lights up, walk the floor to the corral and the plots, walk the auction board up to a price limit and never past it, bid on lots, vote.Every server tick
PlaybookA whole month's plan in one document: the strategy, what to list, which plots to want, the development route, and per good how to trade. The engine always drafts one; Claude may edit it.At the start of each phase

Why it is like this

Heiko decided on 21 October 2026 that real strength comes from a strategist written in code that uses the game's own engine to look ahead, with a small language model making the final call in character. Code does the arithmetic well and costs nothing; the model makes the seat feel like a person. The game must never wait for a model, so if it is slow, broken or out of budget, the strategist's own top pick plays. Also decided that day: light look-ahead only (no deep search), and the model picks from 3 to 5 near-best options.

Personas In the game

A persona is a set of weights that tilt every decision. A weight of 1 is neutral; above 1 that reason matters more, below 1 less, 0 ignores it. The same weights also drive a few direct numbers (how much cash to keep back, how much to pay for a corner).

How it scores a choice In the game

Every option gets a handful of considerations: one fact about the game turned into a score from 0 to 1, with a reason a person can read (“makes about 40 cr a month, costs 175 cr”, “ground not analysed”). The persona's weight raises each score to a power. The scores are multiplied together, with a correction so an option with many considerations is not punished for having them. The result is the option's utility.

Some options are not scored at all because a hard rule forbids them: a MULE the seat cannot pay for without dipping into its cash reserve, a factory it cannot feed, a fifth MULE when its level allows two a month, selling Food it needs. A forbidden option never reaches the shortlist, and the log says why (“Ruled out: it would leave 40 cr, under the 210 cr kept for upkeep”).

The gain curve

Gains in credits are scored on an S-shaped curve: no gain scores 0.5, a gain of one “scale” scores 0.75, a loss of one scale 0.25. The scale differs per decision, because a plot is worth much more over a game than one auction trade.

DecisionGain that scores 0.75
A plot (land plans)1,200 cr
A MULE (development)300 cr
A trade (auction)80 cr

The shortlist and the pick

The options are sorted by utility. The shortlist keeps the best one and any others within the level's tolerance of it, up to the level's maximum (see Levels). The decider picks one. The built-in decider keeps only those within its own, tighter tolerance and draws one at random, each with a chance in proportion to its utility. The draw is seeded from the game, so a replay picks the same, yet the seat has no fixed habit a person could learn and exploit.

Example

Say a Trader-level seat in development has three options left after the hard rules (the numbers are made up to show the arithmetic): a Food MULE on its river plot (utility 0.62), an Energy MULE on the same plot (0.58) and keep the cash (0.41). The shortlist takes options within 0.2 of the best (above 0.496): Food and Energy. The built-in decider takes options within 0.1 of the best (above 0.558): still both. It draws Food with chance 0.62 / 1.20, about 52%, and Energy about 48%. A Tycoon would have kept only Food on its decider list (within 0.04: above 0.595).

Looking ahead: the outlook In the game

To value anything the seat needs next month's prices. The outlook takes a copy of what the seat can see and plays the rest of the month as if nobody builds or trades any more: this month's production, the auction, upkeep (eating, spoilage, the depot limit, the ship), and next month's production. Then it asks the store's own pricing rule what it would post, with or without a few more units of a good. One run of the status quo, no sampling.

For the months after next, it plays the same status quo forward 2 more months (and on to the 6-month horizon to look for shortages). Where that run shows colonists going short of Food or Water, or the corral unable to build its MULEs for want of Smithore, it raises the later price even if today's price has not caught up. A seat at Rookie level does not look ahead: it values everything at today's posted price, which is its built-in mistake.

What a good is worth to it In the game

For each good, the seat builds a list: what each unit it could hold after the auction is worth to it, most valuable first. It keeps two versions: what a unit it already holds is worth (its keep value, the least it will sell for) and what one more unit is worth (its buy value, the most it will pay).

Units it needs

The units next month's needs will use are worth what going short would cost the seat:

GoodWhat going short costs itHow many units count as needed
FoodWalking slower in development. A colonist without Food walks at 0.6 of its pace, so the month's walking takes longer; each second lost is priced at what a second of development is worth to the seat.This month's eating, plus next month's eating grossed up for spoilage
WaterOnly with a Food farm: an unwatered farm makes half. The half lost, at the Food price, spread over the units.As Food, only while it owns a farm
EnergyA unit short keeps on the plot the game would turn off at that point (biggest Energy user first): it is worth what that plot loses turned off, 80% of its output at the store's price after tax, spread over the plot's use. A factory waiting for this month's inputs is covered last, as it runs last. An approximation: it leaves out the inputs a turned-off factory saves.Its plots' use minus what its own Energy plots make after their own 1, grossed up for spoilage
MedicineOnly when the air makes people sick: the chance of falling sick, times the walking lost at half pace.The month's need
Factory inputsA factory's run is worth its output; that worth is shared over its inputs.One run's inputs, grossed up for spoilage

A second of development time is worth 10 cr while the seat has idle land and the money for a MULE, otherwise 3 cr, and never less than what the pub would pay for it.

Spare units

A unit beyond the need is worth what it would fetch next month: the forecast price, less the sales tax, less what spoils by then, less a safety cut of 0.05 per point of the persona's risk weight because the forecast may be wrong. To buy a spare unit just to resell it the seat also needs a reason to speculate: the resale value is multiplied by its speculate weight (at most 1) and cut a further 0.1 per point of risk. Past what the depot keeps (10 units, more for a good that spoils) a unit is worth nothing. A Rookie ignores spoilage, the depot and the air.

Food and Energy the seat needs are never sold at any price. When the store cannot feed the colonists, spare Food and Water held for next month's price are capped at what the store pays now, so the seat sells to the hungry colony instead of hoarding.

The cash reserve

The seat always keeps cash back: 60 cr, plus what next month's Food, Water and Energy shortfall would cost at the store's selling price (but no more than 3 times the reference price per unit, so a price spike does not freeze all its cash), all times its persona's reserve weight. It will not spend into this reserve, except to buy the Food and Energy it needs.

Example: how a computer player values one unit of Food

The Gambler (trader persona, Trader level) in month 3 owns a Food plot and an Energy plot and holds no Food. It has idle land and the money for a MULE, so a second of development is worth 10 cr to it. Its month's walking takes about 20 seconds (WS1 measured about this).

  • It eats 2 + 1 for its crew of 2 = 3 Food a month. The needed block is 3 now plus 3 / 0.5 = 6 for next month (Food spoils by half) = 9 units.
  • Going hungry: 20 s × (1 / 0.6 − 1) = 13.3 s lost, × 10 cr = 133 cr, over 9 units = about 15 cr a unit.
  • Resale: the outlook says Food posts 45 next month. After the 5% tax 42.75, half spoils: 21.4, the risk cut (1 − 0.05) leaves about 20 cr (its keep value). For buying it is cut again (× 1 for speculate, × 0.9 for risk): about 18 cr.
  • Its buy value for each of the 9 needed units is the higher of the two: 18 cr.

The store sells Food at its posted price plus 10%: at 45 posted, about 50. The seat's limit is 18, so it does not buy, and goes hungry with money in the bank (WS9 measured hungry seats holding about 1,200 cr). This is exactly what Heiko saw at the table on 5 November. See Buying what it is short of for the fix being built.

Why it is like this

The design (21 October) asked each unit to be valued by what it does for the seat, not by its market price, so that the seat sells what it does not need and keeps what it does. For Food the only cost of hunger the engine had was slower walking, so that is all the value model counted. Measured on 5 November (WS9, 40 games on each of 3 seeds): a seat came to the Food auction short in 86–87% of its months; in 76–85% it refused on value (its median value 19 cr against store asks of 49–56), although in 99% it could have paid for a unit.

Buying and selling in the goods auction In the game

When a good comes up, the seat makes a plan for it on the status screen and decides there whether to be a buyer, a seller or stay out, and how far to go. Then its hands walk the board.

The decision

  • Sell: sell down to the last unit worth less to it than the sale would bring after tax. A second option keeps a buffer of half next month's need (at least 1 unit) on top.
  • Buy: buy up to the last unit worth more to it than the store's selling price, within its budget for the good. If some of those units are only for resale, a second option buys just what it needs.
  • Pass.

The budget for one good is half its money, and never so much that it dips into the cash reserve. A sale counts as fairly sure (0.9), a buy of what it needs too (0.9), a buy partly to resell less so (0.6), and the persona's risk weight sharpens that. It expects a sale to fetch at least 0.85 of the posted price, or what the store or the ship pays if more.

On the board: the buyer's limit

A buyer's limit for its next unit is

limit = the lower of (what one more unit is worth to it) and (the money it may spend)

where the money it may spend is all its money for Food or Energy it needs, and otherwise what is left of the good's budget. A seller's floor is the keep value of the unit it would give up, raised to cover the sales tax. Both follow its stock as units trade. Then, on every tick:

  1. If the best price on the other side is inside its limit, it walks there and trades.
  2. If a rival on its own side is ahead and the limit leaves room, it overtakes by one credit and a little more.
  3. If the gap to the other side is small (3–8% of the board, times its patience), it waits for them to close it.
  4. Otherwise it edges toward them, never past its limit.

It re-aims only after its reaction time, starts after a delay of 300–1,500 ms times its patience, and wanders its stopping point by up to 3% of the board inside its limit, so its stops are hard to read. At the store's or the ship's line, units trade by themselves; it stays while it still wants units there and steps off after the last.

Cornering and spite

At Trader and Tycoon level, a seat with a corner weight may try to corner a good: when the store holds no more than the other players are short of, and the good spoils less than half a month, it adds 0.5 of next month's price times its corner weight to the value of up to 6 units the others lack. A seat with spite, while the leader is short of the good, adds 0.1 of next month's price times its spite to the next 2 units, to keep them from the leader.

Unclear in the code

Cornering needs a good that spoils less than half a month (STRATEGIST.cornerMaxSpoilage 0.5), and Food spoils exactly half (GOOD_CONFIG.food.spoilage 0.5). So Food is never cornered, although the monopoly plan's list (AI_PLANS.monopoly.goods) names it. Whether that is meant is for WS1 (strategist) and WS9 (playbooks) to say.

What is changing

Heiko's 5 November table notes on the auction itself (slower selling, auto-stop at equilibrium, rest zones) are WS3's; the store's pricing rule is being redesigned by WS5. Both will need the hands checked again. Owner: WS1 for the hands.

Buying what it is short of Being built

Heiko, 5 November, at the table: all the computer players were short of Food while he and the store were selling, and none of them bought. Later two were short of Energy, “which would most likely lead to them not producing anything next month, the worst case”, and stood still in the auction. The PM ruled the same morning: a computer player short of Food or Energy must buy at the store's price when it can afford it. Water was added to the same fix.

What was measured

WS1 measured the Energy case over 5 seeds: 167 seat-months short of Energy. In 47 of them the store was selling and the seat could pay, but did not buy:

For Food the value itself is the problem: median 19 cr against asks of 49 to 56 (see the worked example).

The stopgap

For Food, Energy and Water a computer player works out how much it must hold when the auction ends to be all right next month: fed when next month's development starts, and no plot of its making half for want of Energy or Water at next month's production. It counts what it holds, not what its plots may make. The game eats, drinks and spoils in that order (upkeep straight after the auction), so this is more than the need on the status screen:

GoodEnough for next monthExample
FoodWhat upkeep eats now, plus next month's eating again once half of what is left has spoiled (Food spoils 50%)Eats 3: 3 + 3 / 0.5 = 9
EnergyNext month's need for its plots, once a quarter of what it holds has spoiled (Energy spoils 25%)Needs 4: 4 / 0.75, rounded up = 6
WaterAs Food, with Water spoiling 5%, and only for a player with a Food farm, since an unwatered farm makes half. Without a farm, going short of Water costs it nothing, and it values Water as beforeDrinks 2: 2 + 2 / 0.95, rounded up = 5

The home depot keeps at most 10 units of a good, so it never counts on keeping more than that.

  • When it buys. While it holds less than enough for next month, the store is selling the good and it can pay for a unit, it buys up to enough: at the store's price, or cheaper from a player, never dearer. Its cash reserve and its spending limit per good do not stop it; only its money does. It cannot pass. If its money covers only part, it buys what it can.
  • What it won't sell. While it is short and the store is selling, it sells none of what it needs. Otherwise (it holds enough, or the store has none to sell) it keeps the need on the status screen and sells the rest if the price suits it: for Food, what upkeep is about to eat; for Energy, what its plots will burn, even when its own Energy plots should make that; for Water, what it drinks, but only if it has a Food farm. Without a farm it may sell all its Water.
  • Stopping on time. A seller that reaches the last unit it may sell steps over to the buyers' edge before the next unit can trade, so a buyer bidding up after it cannot take a unit it means to keep.

Example: The Gambler's Food

The Gambler eats 3 Food a month, so enough for next month is 9. The store sells Food at 50.

  • It holds 2 Food and 1,000 cr. It is short by 7, so it buys 7 at the store's line for 350 cr and ends with 9. With only 120 cr it would buy 2 and end with 4.
  • It holds 9 Food and a hungry rival bids 60. It holds enough, so it sells its surplus above the status screen's 3 if 60 is worth more to it than a unit: it may sell down to 3, but never lower.
  • It holds 5 Food and the store is selling. It is short of the 9, so it sells none and buys 4.
  • It holds 5 Food, the store has none to sell and a rival bids 60. It cannot buy, so it keeps the status screen's 3 and may sell the other 2.

Measured on 20 games: a seat short for next month that could pay now always buys (before: it passed in 443 of 485 such months for Food), hungry seat-months fell from 842 to 758, and no seller lets a deal take a unit past where it meant to stop. The cost, on 100 games on each of four settings: colonists go short of Food or Water in 66 to 70% of months (46 to 54% before; 62 to 66% if the computer players kept back only their Food) and Food stands above 3 times its usual price in 93 to 96% of games (78 to 82% before). Players hungry 3 or more months running stay at 99 to 100%: what keeps a seat fed often is not in the store to buy.

Why it is like this

The PM ruled on 5 November, on Heiko's report from the table: a computer player short for next month must buy at the store's price when it can afford it, and treat the shortage as the loss it is. A first version filled only the status screen's need; upkeep then ate it, so the seats went hungry anyway, and the target became enough for next month. Never selling below enough for next month, even with a surplus, kept the seats fed but starved the colony (colonists short in 83 to 86% of months, Food above 3 times its price in 99 to 100% of games). On 5 November Heiko chose “sell surplus”: keep everything while short and the store is selling, otherwise keep what upkeep eats and sell the surplus.

Claiming land In the game

Land plans

At planning the seat weighs up to six land plans and ranks every free plot under each:

PlanWhich outfits count
Take the most valuable landAny
Farms and power first: feed myselfFood, Water, Energy
Mines for the export tradeSmithore, Crystite, Rare earth
Ground for a factoryAlloy, Electronics, Medicine
Grow my own patchAny, with plots next to its own counting double
Fence in the leaderAny, with plots next to the leader's counting (only with spite, Trader level up)

A plot is worth the best gain the seat sees from a MULE on it over the game (the MULE's monthly earnings for up to 6 months, each month counting 0.9 of the one before, less the MULE's and outfit's price, plus 0.6 of the MULE price for what the developed plot adds to the score) plus the 300 the plot itself adds to the score. From Trader level, a Food, Water or Energy MULE that covers the seat's own shortfall counts each such unit at 3 times the reference price at least. Unanalysed ground scores lower (0.8), weighted by risk.

The plans themselves are scored by what their best plots are worth (weighted by greed), whether they feed the seat (weighted by reserve) and whether it has the cash to develop them.

The land grant

The grant is one pass of the light, and each seat may claim 1 plot. When a plot lights up, the seat presses Claim only if, among the free plots still ahead of the light, the lit one is within its claims left plus the rivals still claiming, and its utility is at least 0.75 of the best one ahead. A rival still claiming has claims left and has not pressed Done; both are public on the grant. The seats rank the board nearly alike, so each such rival may take a plot it ranks higher before the light gets there. Early in the pass better plots lie ahead and it waits; late it takes the best that is left. It never presses while it already holds idle plots it cannot pay to develop. It presses after its reaction time plus 150–300 ms for the choice, and a Rookie misses a wanted plot one time in four. When it wants nothing still ahead, it is done with land.

Without counting rivals, four to six seats queued for the same few plots and a loser could end month 1 with no land: 75 seats in 400 games before, none after.

The land auction

For a plot up for auction, the seat bids up to the lowest of: what the plot is worth to it, its money minus the cash reserve, and its money minus the price of a MULE for this plot and each idle plot it already holds. For a MULE lot, up to what a MULE of that outfit gains on its best idle plot. Against a player it overtakes by 10–35% of the gap to its limit; against the colony's proxy bid it walks straight to the colony's cap if that is within its limit, otherwise leaves the lot to the colony. When its money is under its cash reserve it lists one lot a month itself (an idle plot or a MULE, the one worth least to it, never its last Food or Energy MULE) at a minimum of 0.8 of its worth, sold to the store if nobody bids.

Developing land In the game

In development the seat works through a to-do list: analyse the plots worth it, then for each MULE the strategist picks, buy it, outfit it and install it; then fit filters and go to the pub. A MULE it led out of the land auction goes in first.

  • Which MULE: for each idle plot and each outfit, the gain over the game (as for land) minus the walk there at what its time is worth; plus “keep the cash”. Hard rules: the corral has a MULE, the level's MULEs a month are not used up, the walk fits the clock, it can pay without dipping into the reserve, and a factory has inputs for 2 months. A farm for a good it is short of may use the cash kept back for that good. After each MULE it decides again.
  • Analyses: an unknown plot of its own is analysed when its best and second-best uses are within 25% of each other; at most 2 a month (Rookie 1), never with fewer than 20 seconds left.
  • Filters: only once the air is within 10 points of Smoggy, on its own plots putting out more than 1 air point a month, worst first, as far as its Alloy goes.
  • Air: at full scoring each air point a plot puts out costs it 10 cr, rising as the air gets dirtier (doubled by Smoggy).

On the floor: walking, wolves and glints In the game

Development is walked on the arcade floor, and computer players walk it like you: one step of the to-do list at a time, by stick and button, at the same speed (1 times a person's), along the quickest path on the map and through the store's doors. They stand 0.5 s at a counter before pressing. A step fails, and what depends on it is dropped, when its place cannot be reached, the counter offers something else (the corral sold out, money short) or the colonist pushes without moving for 20 ticks.

In politics In the game

Computer players run for office, vote and, as Governor, set the tax, propose laws and spend the colony fund. In short: a persona runs for Governor with a set chance per election (the aggressive seat most often); it votes for itself if it runs, otherwise for the smallest landowner, so the table leans against the leader; in an unhappy colony it will not back the sitting Governor if anyone else runs. As Governor it steers the sales tax toward its persona's target, proposes the law that costs the leader most against itself, pays a general strike and repairs damage when its persona does, and commissions the most prestige the fund can pay for. On a law ballot it votes for a new law when it costs it no more than the table's median, and for a repeal when the law costs it more (a Governor always votes yes, a Rookie flips a coin). The full rules and numbers are in Colony and politics: the computer Governor.

Claude as a player: the decider In the game

A Smart seat asks Claude (the model claude-haiku-5-5) to make three kinds of decision: which land plan to play, which MULE to build, and buy, sell or pass in the auction. Development is walked on the floor at every table today, where the pick stays the built-in one (see On the floor), so in practice Claude decides land and the auction. For each, Claude receives:

  • a fixed primer on how a month goes and how to reply, plus the persona's character card (the same for every call of that persona, so it is cached and cheap);
  • a few lines on the seat's situation: month, money, plots, goods and needs, the MULE price, who looks like the richest rival;
  • the shortlist of up to 5 options, lettered A to E, each with its label, its reasons and its score;
  • the last 6 lines of table talk, each cut to 200 characters and marked as quoted speech.

Claude answers with one letter and one line to say at the table (at most 140 characters, or nothing). It may pick a weaker option when the character would. It can never name an option off the list, never invent a move, and never act on what someone says in chat: table talk is quoted, never an instruction. A reply that is late (the hands wait 2.5 s in development and the auction, 5 s for land), unreadable or off the list is dropped, and the built-in pick plays.

How a computer player will value a good

Every unit it could hold after the auction gets one value:

value = chance it is needed × what going without it costs + chance it is not needed × what it fetches later

  • Needed means the stock that keeps it whole until next month: what it eats and drinks at the month's end, what spoils after that (half its Food, a quarter of its Energy), and what its plots will burn. What its own farms and plants will make counts too, but as less than certain, since an event or a shortage can cut it.
  • Going without is never cheaper than buying the unit back next month. For Energy and Water it is exact: one unit short switches off the next plot in the order the game uses (the biggest user first), and that plot makes only a fifth of its output.
  • What it fetches later is next month's price after the store's cut, the tax and spoilage, and nothing past what its depot keeps.
  • Its cash goes to the most valuable units across the whole month first, so a player with little money buys Energy before a second sack of Food, even though Food is auctioned first.
  • Building uses the same values: a power plant or a well is worth what its output saves. A player short of Energy builds a plant when that beats buying.

Example: a player eats 3 Food a month and has none. Upkeep eats 3, then half the rest spoils, so it needs 9 to stay fed. With Food at 72 and 1,200 cr in hand, each of those 9 units is worth at least 72 to it, so it buys them. Today it values each at about 20 and buys none.

Why it is like this

Measured on today's game (40 games on each of 3 seeds, 5 November): a computer player went into the Food auction short in 86–87% of months, and refused to buy in most of them because it valued a needed unit at about 19 cr against an asking price near 50. It was hungry at 88–89% of developments with about 1,200 cr in hand. Energy shortages cut some production in 40–43% of months. Today a needed Food unit is worth only the walking time hunger costs, which takes no account of the price level. A short buying fix from the Balance team (WS1) covers Food, Water and Energy until this design ships.
