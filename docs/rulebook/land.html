Land and geology

The map, what lies hidden in its ground, and every way land changes hands: the free land grant, the land auction, selling to the store, the geo drone and analysing a plot on foot.

The map In the game

Every game is played on one grid of square plots, made fresh from the game's seed. The same seed always makes the same map, so a game can be replayed. The size depends on how many players sit at the table.

The store sits on the plot at the very centre of the map. It is not land: nobody owns it, claims it or walks across it, and colonists go in and out through the doors on its left and right sides (see the store floor).

A river runs from the top row to the bottom row, always passing right beside the store. It bends from row to row: at least 2 bends on a small map, 3 on a mid map and 4 on a large one, plus 0 or 1 more by chance. It never comes closer than 2 columns to the left or right edge. Where it bends, an extra river plot joins the two rows, so the river is always one connected stream.

The rest of the land is laid out in regions of one terrain, each at least 3 plots big and none larger than 12% of the land beside the river. Each terrain aims at a share of that land, allowed to stray by a third of its target plus one plot, and no terrain may cover more than a third.

What every map promises

The map maker checks each map against a set of rules before play, and repairs the ground until it meets them. So no table is dealt a map without Food, Water, Energy or Smithore.

  • Enough good land. A plot counts as good for Food, Water or Energy at a rating of 1 or more, and for Smithore, Crystite or Rare earth at 3 or more. Every map has at least 10 good Food, 10 good Water, 10 good Energy and 3 good Smithore plots per player.
  • Usually more. Normally the map has at least 12 Food, 13 Water, 13 Energy and 4 Smithore plots per player. With a chance of 25%, one of these four goods is the map's scarce good and only gets the floor above.
  • Nothing out of reach. From any plot, a good Food, Water and Energy plot lies within 3 steps (4 on a large map), and a good Smithore plot within 5 steps (6 on a large map).
  • Smithore comes in seams. At least 70% of the good Smithore plots touch another good Smithore plot.

Terrain and what it hints In the game

Everyone sees every plot's terrain. Terrain does not decide what a plot makes; it only sets the odds for its hidden ground (next section). For each grown good (Food, Water, Energy) a terrain is either often good, neutral or poor; for Smithore it has an odds level from none to very high.

No hint ever names Crystite or Rare earth: they turn up anywhere (see the rings). Terrain also decides how fast a colonist walks across a plot and whether wolves live there; that is in Development.

Why it is like this

The design rule is "what you see is not what you get": terrain tells you the odds, only an analysis tells you the truth. Two hills side by side can be a rich Smithore seam and an empty rock. The hints are kept honest: over many maps, each terrain's average rating must sit within 0.3 of the hint, and no part of the map (an edge row or column, or the middle) may run more than 0.1 poorer or richer than the hints say.

Hidden ground ratings In the game

When the map is made, every plot gets a hidden rating for each of the six raw goods. The rating, not the terrain, is what a MULE on that plot works with. Nobody sees it until they analyse the plot or fly a drone over it.

GoodsRating scaleWhat a MULE makes from it
Food, Water, Energy (grown)-2 to +32 plus the rating, but never less than 1: a poor plot still works, badly.
Smithore, Crystite, Rare earth (mined)0 to 4Exactly the rating. A 0 means nothing to mine: a MULE there makes nothing.

That is the plot's base. Traits, species, special fields, neighbours and pollution then add or take away; see Production.

How grown ratings are dealt

For Food, Water and Energy, each plot's rating is drawn from its terrain's odds row:

The draw is not plot by plot at random. A smooth random "field" is laid over the map for each good, and within each terrain the best ratings go where the field is highest. So good farmland comes in patches rather than speckles. Smithore uses a narrower field, which gathers its rich plots into seams.

Floors and repairs

After the first deal the map maker repairs the ground until the promises in The map hold: it lifts single plots to exactly the "good" threshold, swaps ratings within a terrain to join Smithore seams, and lowers the scarce good on a few plots. It never rerolls the map. Crystite and Rare earth have no floors and no scarcity: their rings replace them.

Crystite and Rare earth: the rings

Crystite and Rare earth ignore terrain completely. For each of the two, the map maker picks 3 or 4 peak plots at random anywhere on the land, then rates every plot by how many steps (across the grid, never diagonally) it lies from the nearest peak:

Steps from the nearest peakRatingPlots in one ring
0 (the peak)41
13up to 4
21 or 2, by chanceup to 8
3 or more0the rest

Rings may overlap each other and the other mineral's rings, and a plot takes its rating from the nearest peak. One full ring holds 5 good plots (rating 3 or more), so a map has roughly 15 to 20 good plots of each, whatever its size. On a small map that is plenty per player; on a six-player map it is about 3 each.

Because no terrain says where the rings are, every plot shows the same hint for both: the map-wide average rating, 0.6 (measured over maps of 2 to 6 players: about 0.82 on small maps, 0.60 on mid and 0.41 on large). The phone's terrain line simply leaves them out.

Example

A Crystite peak lands on a plains plot at column 5, row 3. That plot is Crystite 4. The four plots touching it are Crystite 3, whether they are hills, swamp or river. The eight plots two steps away are each 1 or 2. Everything further out is 0, unless another peak is nearer. A player who analyses one of the 3s and finds Crystite knows a richer plot is next door, and the race is on.

Why it is like this

Heiko, 21 Oct: Crystite and Rare earth come in rings, as in the original, at 3 to 4 random places per map with no tie to terrain or river side: "the whole idea is that you need to analyse the ground and you can be lucky". On hints he chose "Nothing, test to find", and "Rings replace them" for floors and scarcity.

Hidden hazard traits In the game

Each land plot also carries 0, 1 or 2 hidden traits. The count is drawn evenly from the list 0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 2, so half of all plots have none, a third have one and a sixth have two (a repeated draw adds nothing, so some "two" plots end with one).

TraitWhat it does today
Hot springEnergy +2, Water -1 on that plot
ToxicFood -2 on that plot
Thin veinA mine there makes 1 less for every 3 months it has run
Bedrock, Sheltered, Unstable, Infested, Flood plainNothing yet (see below)

You see a plot's traits once you have analysed it, and you always see the traits of plots you own.

What is changing

The design says the other five traits change event risks (floods, quakes and so on). Today no rule reads them: a search of the engine finds them only in the list of trait names. Whether they should do something, and what, is open with the Events team (WS1) and the Maps team (WS5).

Special fields In the game

A few plots carry a special field, drawn on the map for everyone to see, the TV included. Unlike the ground, a special field is public. A map has 3 (small), 4 (mid) or 5 (large) of them, at most 2 of any kind and at least 3 different kinds. They sit at least 4 steps apart, both sides of the river get at least one, and none sits on the river, a lake, the store or a plot touching the store.

Meteor craters

A meteor strike is a production event with a 3.2% chance each month. After the month's goods are made it hits one plot (never the store). The owner keeps the plot, but its MULE, its run of months and its filter are destroyed, any special field there is replaced, and the plot becomes a meteor crater with Crystite 4.

Colony sites

When politics is on, the colony can win a plot at the land auction for a building that needs one (a Park, a Stadium or the Spaceport upgrade). That plot becomes the colony's: it is no player's, cannot be claimed or flown over by the drone, and stands bare. See the colony's bid below and Colony and politics.

The land grant In the game

Every month starts with planning, then the land grant: the colony gives away free land, one plot to each player who wants one.

  • The free plots light up one at a time in reading order: the top row first, left to right, then the next row. Each stays lit for 650 ms.
  • While a plot is lit, the first player to press CLAIM gets it. A press counts only for the plot the player saw lit, so a late thumb never grabs the next one by mistake ("That plot is not lit").
  • Each player has one claim per grant. The button shows "1 OF 1 CLAIMS LEFT" and turns to "Done" once used.
  • A player who wants nothing more presses I'm ready ("You're done with land") and claims nothing more this month.
  • The light passes over the free plots once. There is no clock: the grant ends when every player has claimed or is done, when no free land is left, or when the light has passed the last free plot.
  • Once every person at the table is done, the computer players' presses resolve at once and the light races to the end at an eighth of the step (about 81 ms a plot), so nobody waits.

The "Lit now" panel names the lit plot's terrain, any special field, and its terrain hint; for a plot you have analysed or flown your drone over, it shows the real ratings instead (see the geo drone). Each plot you own adds 300 to your worth, before any MULE on it.

When the last free plot is taken, the game ends 3 months later, or at month 12, whichever comes first (see The game at a glance).

Example

A four-player game, month 1: the map has 134 free plots, so a full pass can take up to 134 × 0.65 = about 87 seconds. Lena wants the river plot in row 2; it lights, she presses, it is hers. Once every human has claimed or pressed I'm ready, the computer players' choices settle in a flash.

Computer players in the grant

A computer player presses for a lit plot only if it is worth at least 75% of the best free plot still ahead of the light, and only with a human-like reaction time, so a quick human can beat it. At the default level (Trader) it reacts in 250 to 400 ms plus 150 to 300 ms to decide, and misses a plot it wanted 5% of the time (Rookie 25%, Tycoon never).

Why it is like this

One pass with no clock (WS3, 28 Oct) means a player who wants no land does not hold the table up, and nobody is rushed. One claim per grant is Heiko's ruling of 5 Nov (it was two); the store still builds two MULEs per player a month, so the corral did not shrink.

What is changing

Being built: with one claim each, computer players all chased the same few best plots and a slow one could end the grant with no plot at all (measured: 25 plotless seats in 200 four-player games, 50 at six players). The fix makes them spread their presses; in 400 test games no seat ended plotless. It is in the Strategy team's PR #366 (WS1), which also fixes the glint grab described in Development.

The land auction In the game

After the grant comes the land auction, where plots and MULEs change hands between players. It happens only in a month where something is up for sale; otherwise the game goes straight to development.

Putting a lot up

In planning, a player can list a plot they own, or the MULE standing on it, from the Sell tool. The listing says:

  • the minimum bid, any whole number of 1 or more (the form starts at 250);
  • what happens if nobody bids that much: Store buys (the store pays a fixed price, shown on the form) or Keep it.
LimitValue
Lots one player may list in a month2
Lots the auction takes in a month, from everyone4
Times one plot may be listed in a month (as plot or as MULE)1

A seller can take a listing back at any time in planning. Listings are made only in planning ("Listings are made in planning").

One lot at a time

Lots come up one after another. Each has three stages:

StageLengthWhat happens
Intro3 sThe plot lights on the map with its seller and minimum. Ends early once every buyer presses Ready to bid.
Walk15 sBuyers walk their bids up. Ends early once every buyer presses Done bidding.
Result2.5 s"Yours!", "Sold to …", "The store bought it" or "Unsold".

Who may bid

Everyone but the seller is a buyer, except a player whose money is below the minimum (shown as passing), and, on a MULE lot, a player already leading a MULE. Each buyer starts at the bottom of the board, below the minimum.

Bidding

Hold up to raise your bid, hold down to lower it, let go to stand. Your bid can never go above the money you hold. The board runs from 0.7 times the minimum to 2 times the minimum, and crossing it takes 7.5 seconds. Once the leading bid passes the top of the board, bids climb twice as fast for the rest of the lot, so a hard-fought lot can still reach its price before the bell.

Soft close: when the lead changes hands with less than 2 seconds left, the clock goes back to 2 seconds, so the bell never decides who wins. These top-ups add at most 6 seconds to one lot. A lot's first leader does not count as a change.

At the bell the highest bid at or over the minimum wins; on a tie, whoever got there first, then seat order.

  • Sold plot: the winner pays their bid and owns the plot at once. Any MULE, filter and run of months on it go with it.
  • Sold MULE: the winner pays and leads the MULE, already outfitted, into development, where they must install it on one of their plots before their turn ends or it runs off (see Development). The seller's plot is left without a MULE.
  • The seller receives the bid less sales tax (5% at the start of a game; the Governor may change it), which goes to the colony fund.
  • Unsold, Keep it: nothing moves.
  • Unsold, Store buys: see Selling to the store.

Example

Brix lists a hills plot at a minimum of 250. The board runs from 175 to 500, so bids climb at about 43 cr a second (325 ÷ 7.5). Ossa reaches 310, Lena stops at 290. At the bell Ossa pays 310; the tax is 16 (5% of 310, rounded), so Brix gets 294.

A MULE under the emission cap

When the colony's emission cap stops new Rare earth or Alloy outfits, a MULE already outfitted for them may still be sold at a lot and installed by its buyer. The cap refuses new outfitting, not moving or installing a MULE that already exists, and the air is no worse for the sale.

Why it is like this

The sale rule is the original's land auction: buyers walk up, the highest bid at the bell wins (WS3 spec, 21 Oct). The soft close and the faster climb were added (AM-6) so that a lot is not decided by who happens to be leading when time runs out. The capped MULE is a PM ruling of 5 Nov.

Computer players at the auction

A computer player never bids past the lowest of its money less its cash reserve, what the lot is worth to it, and (for a plot) its room to develop more land. Overtaking, it jumps a random 10 to 35% of the gap to that limit, so its stops are hard to read. Short of cash, it lists one plot or MULE a month itself, at 80% of what the lot is worth to it, with "Store buys" as its fallback.

Selling to the store In the game

There is no "sell" button for land outside the land auction. The way to get rid of a plot or a MULE for sure is to list it with Store buys: if no bid reaches your minimum, the store pays a fixed price, fixed when you list.

LotThe store paysThen
Plot2/3 of a plot's worth, 300: 200 crThe plot goes back to free land. A MULE on it goes back to the store's corral.
MULEhalf this month's MULE price, roundedThe MULE goes back to the corral; the plot stays yours, bare.

The store always pays, even when its own cash runs below zero, so "Store buys" is a sure exit. The store price is set below a plot's worth on purpose, so selling to the store can never beat simply holding the land at the end.

Example

The MULE price this month is 170. Lena lists the Smithore MULE on her plains plot at a minimum of 120, Store buys. Nobody bids 120. The store pays her 85 (half of 170), the MULE returns to the corral, and her plains plot is bare, ready for a better outfit.

The colony's plot sale In the game

Each month, as the land grant ends, there is a 10% chance that the colony itself puts one free plot up for auction (Heiko asked for a chance between 1 in 10 and 1 in 8). The plot is picked at random among the free plots, and its lot comes up first.

  • Its minimum is 2/3 of a plot's worth: 200.
  • Sold, the money goes to the store's cash, with no sales tax (the colony is not a player).
  • Unsold, it simply stays free land.
  • It never pushes out a player's listing: if 4 lots are already listed, or no land is free, there is no colony lot.

The roll comes from the game's seed and the month alone, so it is the same in a replay.

The colony bids for a site

With politics on, the colony can also buy. When a commissioned building needs a plot and the Governor has set a top bid, the colony bids on every player's plot that comes up: one credit over the best player's bid, up to the lower of the Governor's top bid and what the fund holds above its reserve. A player who reaches that cap takes the lot. If the colony wins, the fund pays the seller (less tax), any MULE on the plot goes back to the corral, and the plot becomes the colony's bare building site.

What the computer players know

Computer players read the game only through the same filter as a phone. For any plot they have not analysed, they see the terrain's average rating (and 0.6 for Crystite and Rare earth on every plot), never the real one, and no traits except on their own plots. They never see another player's analyses, drone orders or drone finds. To learn the truth they must analyse on foot, like a human:

  • they analyse an unknown plot of their own when its best and second-best uses are within 25% of each other on the averages;
  • at most 2 analyses a month (Rookie 1), and never with less than 20 seconds left;
  • when choosing land, an unknown plot counts for less than a known one (they score "known ground" lower when it is a guess from the terrain).

Drone and geology redesign Designed

On 5 Nov Heiko played a game, could not get the drone to work as he expected, and asked for a redesign of how players learn about the ground. In his words, condensed:

  1. Pick, then scan. Mark the plots first, press Scan, and get a detailed report.
  2. Always see your own land. An easy way to look at your plots and their resources at any time, in planning and in development.
  3. Pay to know. Resources show only after you paid for them, by drone or by the assay in development.
  4. A clear marker on plots that hold something special, like Crystite or Rare earth.
  5. One scale. Every resource rated 0 to 3 per plot.

  • My plots. A tool beside Look in planning, and a Plots chip on the walking screen in development: every plot you own, one row each, with its MULE and every good's dots. A plot nobody analysed says how to find out. In the store, the "Your plots" box shows dots instead of words.
  • Analyse says what it found. After Analyse on any plot, not only your own, a card opens beside it for a few seconds with its dots and what it is best for.
  • The gem. On your own map only: a plot you analysed shows its best good as dots, and a gem at its top (beside your mark on your own plot) if it holds Crystite or Rare earth. The TV never shows either.

Why it is like this

Heiko at the table, 5 Nov: "I failed to use the drone … mark two plots first then click on scan and then I would get a detailed report", and "an easy way for me to always look at my lots and see the plots' resources". He chose B over the thinner A because A had no list of your plots. The report sits in the rail, not on the map, because two reports on the map cover a sixth of it on a sideways phone.
