The store and prices

The colony store is the colony's merchant: it sells MULEs, buys what you make and sells what you lack. Nobody sets its prices by hand. Each month it reads how much of every good the colony holds against what the colony will need, and posts each price in one of four steps, from normal to a severe shortage. This chapter explains how, with the numbers.

How the whole colony economy fits together, with diagrams: the colony economy explainer (approved by Heiko, 6 Nov).

The store in one minute In the game

  • One price per good, and two lines around it. Before every goods auction the store posts a price for each good. It sells above that price and buys below it, by margins that depend on how short the good is: 15% either side while supply is normal, up to 10% under and 25% over in a shortage.
  • Prices follow the colony's cover, in steps. Cover is how many months the colony's whole supply of a good (the store's shelf plus every player's depot) would last at next month's demand. While the colony's plots make as much as it needs, or it holds 2 months or more, the good sells at its normal price. With less, the price steps up: 1.25 times normal when tight, 2 times in a shortage, 4 times in a severe one.
  • Up at once, down slowly, never below normal, never above the ceiling. A price goes straight up to its step, comes down at most a quarter a month, and is never posted above 12 times the good's starting price.
  • What players pay each other counts. When players paid each other more than the store asked, or bid past a sold-out store, the store's next price follows them.
  • The colony is self-contained. Nothing is imported. Every unit on the store's shelf was made by a player and sold to the store. The only way out is the export ship, which calls every third month.
  • The store never runs out of money. It buys as much as it wants, limited only by shelf room.
  • Crystite is for export. The colony has no use for it except one prestige building; the store posts it at what the export ship pays.

Why it is like this

Water is kept short on purpose: Heiko ruled on 29 Oct that the colony should feel a coming Water shortage from the first month, so players build wells early. Food 4 a seat in the store, Energy 5 a seat, and each player's 2 Food and 2 Energy are Heiko's numbers of 5 Nov.

The colonists come first

The colonists buy their Food and Water from the store at the end of every month, at its selling price, one unit of each per 20 colonists. The store keeps that much back: at the auction it sells players only what is above its colonists' reserve. In a shortage the shelf therefore runs empty for the players first. The status screen and the TV show the store's stock for sale, after the reserve.

The store's goods spoil slowly

The store has proper storage, so at month end its goods lose only a quarter of the usual spoilage (PRICE.storeSpoilage = 0.25). The loss is rounded so that whole units are lost.

The store's money

The store has its own working money, its capital, separate from the colony fund. It earns from MULEs, outfits and drone fees, from selling goods to players, colonists and the export ship, and from colony plots sold at the land auction; it pays for goods, unsold plots and MULEs it buys, and for wolf pelts. Its buying is never limited by its capital, and the capital may go below zero. It is never shown to players and does not count toward anyone's score.

How the posted price is set In the game

The store posts its prices once a month, as the goods auction opens, after production and after any auction-phase event (a pirate raid or a store fire changes the stock first). The prices stay fixed for the whole auction. The game's opening prices are posted by the same rule before month 1, starting from the normal prices (see the opening prices).

Step 1: next month's demand

For each good the store estimates what the colony will need next month:

The export ship's part: once its wishlist is announced (the month before it calls, and the month it is in port), each wishlist good counts a third of what the ship still wants, and goods not on the list count nothing. Before that, each good the ship might want counts its average share. See the export ship.

Colonists' demand does not bend with price: they pay nothing for their meal (they take it from the shelf, free), so their full need counts whatever the store asks. See the colonists' meal.

The 1.6 Energy for a new MULE is not a setting of its own. It is the average Energy a month of the outfits that buy their Energy from the stock (Food 1, Water 1, Smithore 2, Crystite 2, Rare earth 2, from UPKEEP.energyUse), so it follows when those are tuned. The Energy plant's own 1 is left out, because a plant pays it from its own output. In month 1 of a four-seat game that is 8 new MULEs × 1.6 = 12.8 Energy, plus 2.5 for 100 colonists: 15.3 a month, against the 28 the colony holds (20 in the store and 2 for each player), so Energy opens tight.

Under review: the Energy for new MULEs

Measured with the build, on 40 four-seat games of seed 1 with politics on: counting the Energy new MULEs will need cut the farm-months without power from 40.4% to 31.8%, and the colonists at the end of a game fell from 77.5 to 71.7 on average. The programme manager ruled that it stays; Heiko keeps or drops it at the colony economy's first gate, from runs with and without it.

Step 2: cover, and what the plots make

Supply is everything the colony holds of the good: the store's shelf plus every player's depot. MULEs standing in the corral count as the Smithore they were built from. Cover is supply divided by next month's demand: how many months it would last. A good with no demand has endless cover.

What the plots make is what every worked plot of the good is set to make this month, before any Energy shortfall or event.

Step 3: the step the good is in

The store reads down this table and takes the first row that fits. Each step aims at a price, and sets how far under it the store buys and how far over it the store sells.

The normal price is the good's reference price, which learns from what players pay each other (see reference and off-world prices). Exactly 2 months held is normal, exactly 1 month tight, exactly half a month a shortage. The store remembers the step each good was posted in: it sets that month's margins (see the spread).

Step 4: up at once, down a quarter a month

  • Up: when the step's price is above last month's price, the price goes straight to it.
  • Down: when it is below, the price falls by at most PRICE.fallStep = 25% of last month's price, and never below the step's price. A crisis is felt for a few months after it ends.
  • Never below normal. Whatever the step says, the price is at least the good's normal price, so a full colony never sells cheap; a full store simply stops buying. A good only the ship wants is the exception (see goods only the ship wants).
  • The price is a whole number of credits, and never below 1.

Step 5: what players paid each other last month

During each good's walk the store keeps two prices from the deals between players: the highest any unit fetched, and the highest any unit fetched while the store had none of the good for sale. At the next posting it reads them, after Step 4:

  • A high trade. If players paid each other more than the store's selling price at the time, the price is at least last month's price plus half the gap to what they paid (PRICE.tradePull = 0.5). A trade at or under the store's selling price pulls nothing.
  • Sold out and bid past. If players bought from each other while the store had none for sale, the price is at least high enough that the store's selling price reaches the highest price they paid then. A unit traded before the store's shelf emptied does not count.

The store forgets both once it has read them. A good that was skipped at the auction teaches nothing for next month.

Step 6: the ceiling

No price is posted above PRICE.ceiling = 12 times the good's starting reference price (GOOD_CONFIG), whatever a step, a trade or a sell-out asks: the ceiling always wins. That is Food 360, Water and Energy 300, Smithore 600, Rare earth 720, Alloy 1,080, Crystite 1,200, Medicine 1,440 and Electronics 2,640. The ceiling does not follow the reference price as it learns.

Goods only the ship wants

A good nobody in the colony uses this month, only the export ship, is in the off-world step: the store posts it at the off-world price, however much of it the colony holds. Crystite always is. Electronics, Rare earth and Alloy are not while the colony has colonists: they want Electronics, and with it the Rare earth and Alloy it is made from. The price goes straight up to the off-world price and falls to it by at most a quarter a month; the colony's normal price is no floor for it. High trades, sell-outs (Step 5) and the ceiling still apply. The off-world price moves every month (see how the ship's prices move), and Crystite's posted price follows it.

Why it is like this

Heiko's five rules (6 Nov): the store knows the colony's overall demand and sells at the normal, lowest price while supply covers it; prices rise when stock covers under two months and demand is bigger than production, more in a shortage and more again in a severe one; and a very high trade between players pulls the store's price towards it. From the colony economy explainer he approved counting the colony's stock (shelf plus players) rather than the shelf alone, four steps rather than a smooth curve, a quarter a month down, never below normal, and halfway towards a high trade. The 12 times ceiling, the sell-out rule and counting the Energy new MULEs will need come from the market-maker design he chose on 5 Nov. The opening prices go through the steps since the build (7 Nov): before, Water and Energy could open far under their normal price.

Worth knowing (from reading the code)

  • Colonists' Energy and Medicine count as demand in the price, but colonists only ever buy Food and Water from the store. Their Energy and Medicine raise those prices without taking any units off the shelf.
  • A Medicine works' Food and Water inputs are not counted as demand for Food and Water (Smithore for smelters, and Alloy and Rare earth for Electronics works, are).

Whether these are intended is for WS1 to say; WS5's store-pricing design may take them up.

The arithmetic

Tight. 30 × 1.25 = 37.5, posted 38. The store buys at 38 less 15% = 32.3, rounded down to 32, and sells at 38 plus 20% = 45.6, rounded up to 46.

Severe. 30 × 4 = 120. The store buys at 120 less 10% = 108 and sells at 120 plus 25% = 150.

A high trade. Players paid 95, above the store's selling price of 75, so the price moves halfway from 60: 60 + ½ × 35 = 77.5, posted 78. The store buys at 70 and sells at 98.

Sold out and bid past. In a shortage month at 60 the store ran out of Food, and two players then traded at 90. Next month, still in a shortage, two rules lift the price: the high trade (60 + ½ × 30 = 75) and the sell-out (90 ÷ 1.25 = 72, so the store sells at 90 at least). The higher wins: 75, and the store sells at 94.

Coming back down

After a severe month the farms catch up (16 a month) and the colony holds 30 again. The price falls a quarter a month: 120 × 0.75 = 90, then 67.5 = 68, 51, 38.25 = 38, and then 28.5, which is under the normal price, so 30: normal again five months after the crisis.

The pattern is Heiko's: a shortage shows at once as a higher price on both lines, so selling to the store pays exactly when the colony needs it, and the price stays up for a few months after supply returns, which is when a player holding Food sells well.

How the ship's prices move

As each month opens, before anything else happens, the off-world price of every good the ship buys moves: Smithore, Crystite, Rare earth, Alloy, Electronics and Medicine. Two things move it:

  • A pull back to its usual price. The price keeps 70% of how far it was from its usual price last month, as a ratio: Crystite at 160, usually 90, is 1.78 times its usual price; a month later, before the swing, it is 1.780.7 = 1.496 times, about 135. A high price drifts down and a low one up, over a few months.
  • A random swing, drawn from the game's seed, the month and the good. Most months it is small, now and then large, either way. The same seed always gives the same prices, so a replayed game sees them again.

Then the price is held between its limits and rounded to whole credits.

Measured over 200 simulated four-seat games (2,400 months): Crystite's off-world price was above 90 in half the months and below it in the other half, above 200 in 1.0% of months, 280 or more in 0.1%, and 50 or less in 2.9%. It reached both limits, 40 and 300, and never passed them.

Example: Crystite at 160

Crystite's off-world price stands at 160. With no swing, next month it would be 90 × (160 ÷ 90)0.7 = 134.6, and in three months 90 × (160 ÷ 90)0.7 × 0.7 × 0.7 = 109.6. A computer player's forecast follows exactly that path, without the swings, and reads it rounded down: 134 next month and 109 in three. The real price then swings above or below it. Holding Crystite when its price is high is a bet the price will not fall back before the ship calls.

How the reference price learns

Once a month, when each good closes at the auction (or is skipped), its reference price moves:

new reference = reference + (implied − reference) × 0.25 + (off-world − reference) × 0.1

  • implied is the average price players paid each other for the good this month, divided by the step it was posted in this month: by 1 when normal or off-world, 1.25 when tight, 2 in a shortage, 4 in a severe one. A trade at a shortage price says little about the normal price, so it counts only for what players paid above the step. With no trades between players, implied is the reference itself and only the pull toward the off-world price is left.
  • Trades with the store and the ship teach nothing: they happen at the store's and the ship's own prices.
  • The result is rounded to whole credits, and is never below 1. There is no other bound.

Example

Paying above normal. Alloy, reference 90, in the normal step. Players pay each other 130 on average. Implied is 130, so the reference becomes 90 + 40 × 0.25 + 0 = 100. Next month the pull alone takes it to 99.

Trading in a shortage. Food is in the shortage step, twice normal: 60. Two players trade Food at 70. Implied is 70 ÷ 2 = 35, so the reference becomes 30 + (35 − 30) × 0.25 = 31.25, rounded to 31: they paid a little more than the shortage alone explains. Had they traded at 60, the reference would not move.

Worth knowing (from reading the code)

Because the reference is rounded every month, the pull toward the off-world price stops a few credits short: a gap of 5 credits or less above it (4 or less below it) moves by less than half a credit and rounds back. A reference that drifted to 34 for Food stays at 34 until players trade Food with each other again. WS1 owns the rule; the store-pricing design may change it.

When a buyer and a seller meet between the store's lines, both do better than with the store. The store's lines are the fallback: a seller can always sell while the store wants the good, and a buyer can always buy while it has some for sale.

  • With nothing for sale, the store shows no selling price ("out").
  • When it wants no more, it shows no buying price.

How the store restocks In the game

Nothing is ever imported. The store's shelf fills in only two ways: players sell to it at the auction, and goods it already holds. It empties when players and the colony fund buy from it, when colonists take their Food and Water at month end, when goods spoil, when it sells its surplus to the export ship, when it builds MULEs from Smithore, and when an event strikes.

How much it buys

At each good's auction the store wants enough to fill its shelf to PRICE.storeMaxCover = 3 months of next month's demand, plus, for a good the export ship takes, a full ship's load to sell when the ship calls. Once it has bought that many its buying line goes. Its money never limits it.

Example

Food demand is 13 a month and the store holds 12. It will buy up to 13 × 3 − 12 = 27 Food at this auction. For Crystite in a four-seat game in a month when the ship has not announced its wishlist, demand is 4 (the ship's average) and a ship's load is 12, so the store wants up to 4 × 3 + 12 = 24 Crystite, less what it holds.

Its buying line is its step's own, for Food and Water as for every good: colonists pay nothing for their meal, so nothing caps what the store pays (in a severe shortage, Food 108 and Water 90). Its selling price is always at least one credit above what it pays, so a player can't buy from it and sell straight back. The earlier restocking bid (1.5 times the posted price whenever the store's shelf held under a month of its colonists' need) is gone: the steps raise both lines instead.

The colonists take their meal at month end

At upkeep the colonists take 1 Food and 1 Water per 20 colonists from the store's shelf, free, as far as it holds them (see the colonists' meal). Only a short shelf leaves them hungry; the price never does. A fed colony grows 4% a month; a hungry one shrinks 5%. A month the store couldn't feed its colonists is recorded as a shortage, which puts that good in the severe step the next month, whatever the colony holds.

The MULE price and the corral In the game

A MULE costs its labour plus the Smithore it is built from, times a scarcity factor:

MULE price = (120 + 1 × Smithore's posted price) × scarcity factor, rounded.

  • Labour is MULE.labour = 120 credits; each MULE takes MULE.smithorePerMule = 1 Smithore. So whoever mines Smithore sets the price of MULEs, as in the original.
  • The scarcity factor starts at 1. Each month the corral sold out during development, it goes up by MULE.scarcityUp = 25%. Each month at least half the corral's MULEs were left over (MULE.surplusShare), it goes down by MULE.surplusDown = 10%, never below MULE.minFactor = 0.6. It compounds, so a long MULE shortage gets steadily dearer, but never above MULE.maxFactor = 4: a MULE costs at most four times its labour and Smithore.
  • Gearlings pay 95% of the price for MULEs and outfits (SPECIES_PERK.gearlingPrice).

Example

Smithore is posted at 50: a MULE costs 120 + 50 = 170 (a Gearling pays 162). The corral sells out: next month it costs 170 × 1.25 = 213. It sells out again: 170 × 1.25 × 1.25 = 266. In a month with half the MULEs left over, the factor drops by 10% instead. After seven sold-out months in a row the factor would be 4.77, so it stops at 4: with Smithore still at 50, a MULE costs at most 680. Even with Smithore at its ceiling of 600, a MULE never costs more than (120 + 600) × 4 = 2,880.

In month 1 of a four-seat game, reading the code: the colony holds 20 Smithore in the store plus 10 MULEs in the corral (counted as Smithore), against a demand of 10 (8 MULEs for a month's new land and 2 for the export ship's average), so cover is 3, Smithore opens at its normal price of 50, and the first MULEs cost 170.

Why it is like this

Heiko, 7 Nov: he likes the MULE price's extremes. A cap alone barely shows them: at a 15% climb the factor needs 8 sold-out months in a row to reach 3, so 3× or 4× changed 2 games of 40, late. A quarter a month up to 4× brings a MULE of 500 credits or more into about 1 game in 8 (the dearest, 1,344, in 40 four-seat games of seed 1) while no game ends with MULEs worth 10 times their month-1 price, as 18 of 40 did before the store's price ceiling.

The corral

At every month end the store builds MULEs from its own Smithore, one Smithore each, up to its corral target: 2 MULEs for every seat (a month's new land, by grant and land auction) plus 2 spare (MULE.corralPerPlayer, MULE.spareInCorral). That is 6 at 2 seats, 10 at 4 seats and 14 at 6 seats. Without enough Smithore it builds fewer, and the corral may sell out.

The export ship In the game

The colony's only outlet. The export ship calls every GAME.exportShipEvery = 3 months: in a 12-month game, in months 3, 6, 9 and 12.

Its wishlist

For each call the ship wants EXPORT_SHIP.wishlist = 3 goods, drawn from the game's seed from the six it ever buys. Food, Water and Energy don't travel, so the ship never wants them. The wishlist is announced the month before it calls (months 2, 5, 8, 11), and from then on what it wants counts as demand in the store's prices, so the wishlist goods' prices rise ahead of the ship.

How many it wants of each is EXPORT_DEMAND × seats × 3 months, rounded up:

What it pays

  • The month's off-world price for its first unit of a good (see how the ship's prices move).
  • EXPORT_SHIP.stepDown = 2% less for each further unit of that good, as its hold fills.
  • 10% more from a colony whose air is clean (POLLUTION.cleanExportBonus).
  • Rounded down to whole credits.

Players sell to it first, at the auction in the month it is in port, where it stands on the board as an amber line. At that month's upkeep the store sells the ship its own surplus, everything above 2 months of demand, while the ship still has room. Then the ship leaves and the next call is planned.

A player's sale to the ship pays the sales tax, and the export tax when that law is in force (10%, 20% or 30%), taken after the sales tax. The store's sales to the ship pay no tax; they go into its capital.

Example: 24 Crystite

A four-seat colony with clean air; the ship wants 24 Crystite at an off-world price of 100. The first unit fetches 110, the second 107, the third 105, … the 24th 59. All 24 fetch 2,023 credits before tax. With dirty air the same 24 fetch 100 down to 54, 1,847 in all. At the 5% sales tax a seller keeps about 95% of that.

Why it is like this

The design doc: the ship has limited cargo space and every extra unit of a good sells for a little less, so flooding the ship with one good lowers what each unit earns. The clean-air bonus rewards a colony that keeps its air clean.

Crystite In the game

Crystite is a pure off-world luxury. Mining it costs a Crystite outfit (100 credits) and 2 Energy a plot a month, and it never spoils.

  • No colony use in the price. Nobody in the colony consumes Crystite, so its colony demand is 0; only the export ship's demand counts, and a waiting Observatory's (below).
  • One building needs it. The Observatory, a colony prestige object, needs 2 Crystite and 350 credits. The colony fund buys missing goods for a project from the store at the store's selling price. While the Observatory waits, the Crystite it still needs counts in the store's demand (G-5 C49; see Commissioning).
  • Its price is the off-world price. Because only the ship wants it, it is in the off-world step: the store posts it at the off-world price, which moves every month between 40 and 300 and is usually about 90 (see how the ship's prices move), however much Crystite the colony holds. The price falls to a lower off-world price by at most a quarter a month. Only a high trade between players or a sell-out (see Step 5) can lift it above that. A waiting Observatory takes Crystite out of the off-world step until its Crystite is in: the colony now wants some, so the store prices it by the colony's months of cover, like any good the colony uses.
  • The store buys it only for a ship. In a month when the ship has announced a wishlist without Crystite, and in the month it calls without wanting Crystite, the store wants none: nobody buys Crystite and the good is skipped. In the other months the store stocks up to a ship's load.
  • Selling to the ship. In port with Crystite on its list, the ship pays that month's off-world price for the first unit (10% more from a clean colony), 2% less for each after. Holding Crystite for a better month is a bet.
  • Pirate raid. With a chance of 1 in 100 each month, as the auction opens, pirates take all the Crystite, the store's and every player's, before prices are posted. Nothing protects it today.

Crystite's first months

In month 1 the colony holds no Crystite, and the store opens it at its off-world price of 90: it buys at 76 and, with none on its shelf, shows no selling price. From month 2 it follows the off-world price as it moves, for as long as only the ship wants it and no trade lifts it. Electronics, which the colonists want, opens instead at four times its normal price, 880 (see the colonists' Electronics).

Why it is like this

Heiko, 5 Nov: "Please confirm crystite is a pure luxury good, it has no purpose, correct? crystite is there for export only". The PM confirmed from the code: colony demand 0, the price capped at the off-world price, the off-world price then fixed at 100; the one colony use is the Observatory's Crystite (3 then; 2 since pace set C halved the buildings, Heiko, 6 Nov). Heiko wanted Crystite's off-world price to vary from 40 to 300, usually about 90, with the higher prices more and more unlikely (5 Nov): since PR-4 it does.

The store-pricing design: Heiko's five rules In the game

Heiko approved this on 6 Nov, with the whole colony economy (all 30 recommendations of the colony economy explainer). It replaces the first market-maker design he chose on 5 Nov: the 12x ceiling, the sell-out rule, the moving ship prices and the MULE cap stay; the monthly price steps, the shelf lean and the glut step go. The five rules are in the game: the four steps and how a step is picked, how the price moves, the margins by step, the sell-out rule, the high-trade pull, the ceiling, one selling price on every screen, the opening prices and the Energy new MULEs need. The sections above describe them. The rest of the design is not in the game yet; each part below says who builds it.

Heiko's five rules, close to his words (6 Nov): (1) the store knows the colony's overall demand; when overall supply covers it, it sells at the lowest, normal price. (2) If stock covers less than two months and demand is bigger than production, prices rise. (3) In a shortage they rise more dramatically. (4) In a severe shortage more again. (5) If the last trade between players was very high, above the store's price, the store's price moves towards it. "Can be a function, but also a function with steps."

Ships as one package

Heiko's decision 1 (6 Nov) binds the colony economy together: the colonists' hardship and sickness ship together with the Food and Medicine supply change, tuned in one pass, so players first meet hardship in a colony that can be fed. The colonists' side is the Politics team's (WS7): Hunger, hardship and health.

What you see at the table In the game

  • While the colony holds enough of a good, or makes as much as it needs, the store sells it at its normal price (Food 30) and never lower, however full the shelves are. A full store simply stops buying.
  • When the colony eats more than it makes and holds under two months of it, the store's price steps up the next month: a little when it is tight, sharply in a shortage, very sharply in a severe one. Both lines move: in a shortage the store also pays much more when you sell it Food.
  • When players have just paid each other more than the store asked, the store's price jumps towards that.
  • Once the colony holds enough again, the price comes down a quarter a month.
  • The phone's market strip, the auction and the TV all show the same store price.
  • The export ship's prices move every month: Crystite usually about 90, between 40 and 300; the other export goods about half as much (see how the ship's prices move).
  • However often the corral sells out, a MULE never costs more than four times its labour and Smithore (see the MULE price).

What the colony needs: the demand the store counts Designed, not in the game yet, WS5 and WS7

Once a month, for each good, the store adds up what the whole colony needs in a month:

  • Every player's household and crews: 2 Food and 1 Water a household, plus what each kind of plot uses (see What each kind of plot uses).
  • The Energy their plots burn, by the same table.
  • The colonists' full need, at any price: their Food, Water, Energy and Medicine (in the game since PR-2: colonists pay nothing, so nothing caps their part), and the Electronics they want (PR-3).
  • A festival's Food and the export ship's take, as today.

The colony's stock is the store's shelf plus what every player holds, as today: a colony whose players hold Food is not short (Heiko's decision 9; counting the shelf alone measured worse on every count).

Example: four farmers and 100 colonists

Four players each work three farms and a well: each household and its crews eat 3 Food. 100 colonists eat 5. The store counts 4 × 3 + 5 = 17 Food a month, whatever the price. The colonists' 5 already count in full today; the households' 3 each come with PR-6.

Measured over ten simulated games, the demand the store counts rises from 18.1 to 19.3 Food, 12.8 to 16.6 Water and 17.4 to 18.6 Energy a month. Water rises most, to about 20 by the end of a game, as farms and plants spread.

The colonists' full need with no wages: in the game (the Politics team, WS7, PR-2). Designed, not in the game yet: what each kind of plot eats and drinks, the Maps team (WS5), PR-6.

Medicine Designed, not in the game yet, WS5 and WS7

Medicine. Colonists take their Medicine from the store at the month end, free, after their Food and Water: one a month for every 50 colonists, half as much again in dirty air, and up to 40% more while many of them are sick. Hunger raises the need only through sickness: hungry colonists fall sick more easily, and the sick share sets the extra. There is no separate extra for hunger. It counts in what the colony needs, so a Medicine shortage raises its price through the same four steps as Food: up to 480 in a severe shortage, never above 1,440. Before the steps, Medicine's price climbed without limit: it reached 15,584 in one simulated game.

Superseded: decisions 14 and 15

Superseded by the health rule (Heiko, 6 Nov), settled. Decision 14 (hunger raising the colonists' Medicine need by up to half again) and decision 15 (missing Medicine counting at half weight in the colonists' hardship) are replaced: hunger makes colonists sicker, sickness raises the Medicine need and lowers the mood, and missing Medicine reaches the mood only through sickness, so each effect counts once. See the colony health gauge.

Designed, not in the game yet. The colonists' Medicine with the health rule: the Politics team (WS7), PR-9, and the Maps team (WS5), PR-10.

The colonists' Electronics In the game

Colonists want one Electronics a month for every UPKEEP.colonistsPerElectronics = 50 of them, from month 1, whether or not anyone makes it, and at any price. The store counts that want in next month's demand, and it counts the same number again for Rare earth and for Alloy, one of each for every Electronics, as the Electronics works' recipe takes them. The want is not rounded: 75 colonists want 1.5 a month.

So while nobody makes Electronics, the colony holds none and its price sits at four times normal, 880: the store pays 792 for a unit whose inputs cost about 175 at normal prices, or about 700 if all three are in a severe shortage. An Electronics works pays well, and the Rare earth and Alloy it needs are dearer too, so mines and smelters pay. The 12x ceiling (2,640 for Electronics) only bounds what nothing else would.

Example: 200 colonists

200 colonists want 200 ÷ 50 = 4 Electronics a month. The store counts 4 more Electronics, 4 more Rare earth and 4 more Alloy than before, on top of the Electronics works and the export ship. With no colonists it counts none of the three for them.

The colonists do not take Electronics from the store yet: the want raises prices but takes no units off the shelf. Their mood bonus when they get it is designed (the Politics team's: Electronics, a comfort).

Nothing is imported (Heiko, 6 Nov, again). The colony makes its own goods: colony buildings get their Alloy and Electronics only through the store, from goods players made in the colony and sold to it. A building waiting for Electronics waits for a works to run.

Why it is like this

Heiko, 6 Nov: the price is the signal. Counting the want only once someone makes Electronics would leave its price at the off-world 220 and nobody would start. Decision 27 sends the want down the chain: measured, a high Electronics price alone changed nothing, because nobody mined Rare earth or made Alloy, so a works had nothing to run on.
