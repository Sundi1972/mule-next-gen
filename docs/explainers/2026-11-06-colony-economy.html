How the colony eats, and what the store charges

Four parts that work as one: what each kind of plot uses, what the whole colony needs, how the store prices a good from that, and how colonists feel when they go short. 12 decisions for you are at the end, each with a recommendation.

What you'll see at the table

  • Every kind of plot has its own appetite. Miners eat more, farms and power plants drink, works burn extra Energy. A player with a big estate buys more Water than before; Food stays at about today's amount.
  • The store knows what the whole colony needs: players, their crews and the colonists. While the colony holds enough, it sells at the normal price (Food 30) and never below.
  • When the colony eats more than it makes and holds under two months of a good, the store's price steps up: a little when it's tight, sharply in a shortage, very sharply in a severe one. In a shortage the store also pays much more when you sell it Food.
  • If players pay each other far more than the store asked, the store's price jumps towards that.
  • Once the colony holds enough again, the price comes down a quarter each month.
  • The phone's market strip, the auction and the TV all show the same store price.
  • Colonists never pay. They take their Food and Water from the store's shelf, free. The store already keeps a month of their need back from the players, so they come first. The month-end line says how much they got: "The store fed the colony's 400 colonists 19 of 20 Food and all their Water." A dear Food price never starves them while the shelf has Food.
  • A small miss is shrugged off. Missing 1 Food of 20 changes nothing: the mood holds and the colony still grows. Today that same miss costs 5 mood points and 5% of the colonists.
  • A big miss bites at once, and harder the bigger it is. Half rations: the mood drops 4 that month, from Content 60 to Restless 56, and the colony shrinks 5%. No Food at all: the mood drops 13 in one month and the colony shrinks 10%. Today every miss costs the same 5 points and 5%, whether one unit was missing or all of them.
  • The colony remembers. After a hungry month, feeding them again lifts the mood, but slowly: for a few months it aims a little lower than before. Hunger month after month piles up. Half rations every month settle the mood around 54, Restless; no Food at all keeps it falling about 3 points a month, into Unhappy by month 4.
  • Hard times make trouble likelier. While the memory of hunger lasts, riots, strikes and emigration waves come up more often, up to one and a half times their usual odds.
  • The colony's size follows the shortfall. Fed, or missing up to a tenth, it grows 4% a month. Short of more than that, it shrinks by a tenth of the share missing: 2% at a fifth short, 10% with nothing to eat. The mood adds a little on top: a thriving colony draws settlers, an unhappy one loses them.

First: the colony is underfed today

In ten simulated games of today's colony, the store runs out of Food for players in about nine months of ten, and colonists go short of Food or Water in 91 of 120 months. Every rule below reads that shortage more honestly than today, so on today's supply each of them makes the colony look worse, not better. That is why the first decision asks to bring the hardship rules in together with more supply, tuned in one pass.

Neither the use table nor the new prices cause that, and neither fixes it: it is how much Food the colony grows, which is a separate decision on supply.

How the store sets its price

Once a month, for each good, the store compares what the colony holds with what it needs in a month: its months of cover. It also checks whether the colony makes as much as it eats. If it does, or if the colony holds two months or more, the price is normal. Otherwise the price steps up by how little is left.

Coming back down

How colonists feel when they go short

Three things decide it, each a number that can be tuned:

  1. A tolerance. Missing up to a tenth of what they need costs nothing.
  2. A curve. Past the tolerance the cost grows faster than the shortfall: a little short is a nudge, very short is a blow. The worse of Food and Water counts, because a colony with all its Water and no Food is starving.
  3. A memory. Each hungry month adds to a memory of hardship; each fed month halves it. The memory keeps the mood aiming lower and makes unrest likelier. The same shortfall month after month settles the memory at twice that month's share of hardship.

The mood still drifts at most 5 points a month toward where it is heading, as today. That keeps tax, air and prestige changes gentle. A hungry month's own blow lands at once, outside that limit, the way an event's does; otherwise any shortfall from 40% up would look the same.

Decisions for you

1 · Bring in hardship together with the supply fix, or on its own first?

Today's simulated colony is short of Food or Water in 91 of 120 months, more than half short in 79. Any rule that counts how short they went makes that colony unhappier than today's flat 5 points does.

  • Together with the new consumption and demand, tuned in one pass. For: players first meet hardship in a colony that can be fed, so a hungry month means something. Against: hardship waits for the supply work.
  • On its own, now. For: the colony's real hunger shows at once. Against: most months would read Unhappy until supply catches up, and players would learn to ignore the mood.

Recommended: together.

2 · How hungry are crews?

  • The table above. Each kind of plot has its own needs; Water demand rises by about a third; Food stays at today's rate. For: no new hunger on a colony that is already short. Against: crews are a steady, not a steep, Food demand.
  • Hungrier crews (mines and works eat three quarters of a Food each, Crystite miners a whole one). For: crews are clearly the colony's main demand. Against: without more Food supply it deepens a shortage that already shows in nine months of ten.

Recommended: the table above, and revisit it with the Food supply numbers.

3 · What counts as the colony's stock?

  • The store's shelf plus what players hold. For: it is the overall supply you described; a colony whose players hold Food is not short. Against: a player hoarding Food keeps the price normal while others go short.
  • The store's shelf only. For: exactly what the store can see. Against: the store usually holds less than a month while players hold the rest, so prices would sit in the shortage bands most months.

Recommended: shelf plus players. We simulated both: counting only the shelf was worse on every count, including the colonists' Food.

4 · Steps or a smooth curve?

  • Four steps (the chart above). For: easy to say at the table: "Food is short". Against: the price jumps at a step's edge (38 to 60).
  • A smooth curve through the same points. For: no jumps. Against: no name for the state the colony is in.
  • A rise each month while it lasts (+15%, +40%, +80% a month by band). For: a long shortage keeps climbing. Against: the price depends on history, is hard to predict, and overshoots when supply returns.

Recommended: four steps.

5 · How fast does the price come down?

  • A quarter a month (120 to 30 in five months). For: a crisis is felt for a while, then forgotten. Against: Food stays dear a few months after the farms recover.
  • Half the gap a month (about four months).
  • Straight back to normal. For: simplest. Against: no memory of the crisis; the price snaps.

Recommended: a quarter a month. The chart above shows all three.

6 · Can a glut push the price below normal?

  • Never: normal is the lowest price, as you put it; a full store simply stops buying. For: your rule as written; one rule fewer. Against: players who over-produce feel it only when the store stops buying, not in its price. This drops yesterday's "glut step" (after the store stopped buying, its next buying price was lower).
  • A surplus step at 80% of normal when the colony holds three months or more. For: a gentle signal to stop over-producing. Against: a second rule beside your five.

Recommended: never below normal.

7 · How hard does a high trade between players pull the store's price?

  • Halfway to what players paid (a shortage price of 60 after a trade at 95 becomes 78).
  • All the way (the store sells at what players paid). Against: one desperate buyer sets the whole colony's price.
  • A quarter of the way. Against: barely noticed.

Recommended: halfway. If the store had sold out while they traded, it opens next month selling at what they paid, as agreed yesterday.

8 · Do colonists eat free in every game, or only with politics on?

Colonists eat in every game; the mood exists only when politics is on. When we built the colony we promised a game with politics off plays exactly as before.

  • Every game. One way of feeding colonists everywhere: no wages, no price cap, and the colony grows or shrinks by its shortfall in every game. The mood, its memory and the unrest odds stay politics-only. For: one rule to learn and test; the computer players reason about one kind of demand. Against: games with politics off change too (their colony's size and the store's prices), so the "exactly as before" promise is narrowed to "no mood, prestige or social events".
  • Politics on only. For: politics-off games stay exactly as they are. Against: two ways of feeding colonists in one game engine and in the computer players' reasoning; a player who switches modes meets different hunger rules.

Recommended: every game. You asked for colonists with no economy; keeping wages alive in one mode keeps the thing you asked to remove.

9 · How should the mood react to a shortfall?

  • Curve, memory and an immediate blow (the rule above). For: a bigger shortfall hurts more in the same month, and a long one keeps hurting; the charts above are this rule. Against: the most numbers to tune (seven); in today's underfed colony it makes most months Unhappy (see the simulation).
  • Curve only, inside the 5-point monthly limit. For: simplest, half the work, and the gentlest in today's underfed colony. Against: every shortfall from 40% up costs the same 5 points a month (the orange line), so it brings back today's flat cliff, only at 40% instead of 1 unit.
  • Lift the 5-point limit. For: the curve shows in full at once. Against: the limit also governs tax, air and prestige, so a tax change would jolt the mood too.

Recommended: curve, memory and an immediate blow. It is the only one where "the worse the crisis, the more unhappy they get" shows in a single month.

10 · Keep the 5-point monthly limit on the mood?

  • Keep it for the slow drift; let hunger land outside it. For: tax, air and prestige stay gentle and readable; hunger can still hit hard. Against: two ways the mood moves, which the month-end line has to explain.
  • Drop it. For: one way the mood moves. Against: every input swings at once; a 1% tax change could move the mood by several points in a month.

Recommended: keep it for the drift. Events already land outside it, so hunger joins a pattern players have already seen.

11 · The starting numbers

Tolerance: a tenth. The curve's bend: 1.5. The blow: 8 points at no Food. The memory: 6 points lower aim per unit, halved each fed month, capped at 2. Unrest odds: up by a quarter per unit of memory. The colony shrinks by a tenth of the share missing. A harsher first set (a blow of 15, 12 points per unit, odds up by half) sent 8 of 10 simulated games into Unrest; this set is the one that did not.

  • Take them as starting values, to be tuned with the rest of the colony's numbers at the end. For: builds can go ahead now. Against: early playtests feel these values.
  • Gentler or harsher now. For: you set the feel directly; the first chart shows what each shortfall costs. Against: tuning before the simulation has measured the whole package.

Recommended: take them as starting values.

12 · The store loses the colonists' payments

Colonists paid the store for their Food and Water; now they don't. You ruled in October that the store's money is unlimited and never shown, so the store's buying is not affected.

  • Nothing replaces the payments. For: it is what "no economy" means; nothing the players see changes. Against: none at the table; the store's hidden running total just stops receiving them.
  • An off-world grant pays the store for what the colonists take. For: keeps the store's books as they were. Against: money that nobody sees, for no effect.

Recommended: nothing replaces them.
