How the colony eats, and what the store charges

Four parts that work as one: what each kind of plot uses, what the whole colony needs, how the store prices a good from that, and how colonists feel when they go short. 30 decisions for you are at the end, each with a recommendation.

What you'll see at the table

  • Every kind of plot has its own appetite. Miners eat more, farms and power plants drink, works burn extra Energy. A player with a big estate buys more Water than before; Food stays at about today's amount.
  • The store knows what the whole colony needs: players, their crews and the colonists. While the colony holds enough, it sells at the normal price (Food 30) and never below.
  • When the colony eats more than it makes and holds under two months of a good, the store's price steps up: a little when it's tight, sharply in a shortage, very sharply in a severe one. In a shortage the store also pays much more when you sell it Food.
  • If players pay each other far more than the store asked, the store's price jumps towards that.
  • Once the colony holds enough again, the price comes down a quarter each month.
  • The phone's market strip, the auction and the TV all show the same store price.
  • Colonists never pay. They take their Food and Water from the store's shelf, free. The store already keeps a month of their need back from the players, so they come first. The month-end line says how much they got: "The store fed the colony's 400 colonists 19 of 20 Food and all their Water." A dear Food price never starves them while the shelf has Food.
  • A small miss is shrugged off. Missing 1 Food of 20 changes nothing: the mood holds and the colony still grows. Today that same miss costs 5 mood points and 5% of the colonists.
  • A big miss bites at once, and harder the bigger it is. Half rations: the mood drops 4 that month, from Content 60 to Restless 56, and the colony shrinks 5%. No Food at all: the mood drops 13 in one month and the colony shrinks 10%. Today every miss costs the same 5 points and 5%, whether one unit was missing or all of them.
  • The colony remembers. After a hungry month, feeding them again lifts the mood, but slowly: for a few months it aims a little lower than before. Hunger month after month piles up. Half rations every month settle the mood around 54, Restless; no Food at all keeps it falling about 3 points a month, into Unhappy by month 4.
  • Hard times make trouble likelier. While the memory of hunger lasts, riots, strikes and emigration waves come up more often, up to one and a half times their usual odds.
  • The colony's size follows the shortfall. Fed, or missing up to a tenth, it grows 4% a month. Short of more than that, it shrinks by a tenth of the share missing: 2% at a fifth short, 10% with nothing to eat. The mood adds a little on top: a thriving colony draws settlers, an unhappy one loses them.

First: the colony is short of power, and that empties its farms

Two teams measured today's colony over ten simulated games and found the same thing. It has enough farms and wells; it is short of Energy. There are no power plants until about month four, the colony is 40% short of Energy over a game, and 7 to 11 plots stand switched off every month from then on. When the power runs out, wells are switched off before farms, so the Water goes next, and then the farms, short of both, make about a quarter of what they could: a median farm makes 1 Food a month where it could make 4. By month twelve the colony has 14 farms where 6 would feed everyone if they were powered and watered.

On top of that, Food loses half its weight every month in a player's stock, and a player counts as hungry when they hold too little after that loss, so most "hungry" months are spoilage, not starvation. And colonists still pay wages today: late in a game they go short beside a store holding Food they can't afford.

So every rule below reads a real shortage more honestly than today, and on today's power supply each of them makes the colony look worse, not better. That is why the first decision asks to bring the hardship rules in together with more supply, tuned in one pass, starting with power.

Crews that go short

Today a crew only eats; nothing happens to it if its player leaves it short. The proposal gives each player's crews last month's meal as their state: crews eat at the month's end, after the household, from their own player's stock, and next month every plot of that player works at the share below. A power plant keeps running, as today. The farm's “dry” rule goes: the farm's own Water in the table is its irrigation, and a thirsty crew works slower on every plot instead of only farms halving.

Why last month's meal: a plot works before the month's market, so a crew can only be fed from what its player kept back, and today's rule, which looks at the stock just after the month's eating and spoilage, finds nearly everyone short.

How the store sets its price

Once a month, for each good, the store compares what the colony holds with what it needs in a month: its months of cover. It also checks whether the colony makes as much as it eats. If it does, or if the colony holds two months or more, the price is normal. Otherwise the price steps up by how little is left.

Coming back down

The colony level: what a shortage does to the colonists

What happens today

  • Food and Water are the only goods colonists consume. A short shelf makes the colony shrink 5% instead of growing 4%, and costs the mood 8 points of its aim per good short. (The colonists' hardship proposal makes both depend on how short they went.)
  • Energy and Medicine do nothing to the colonists. Their only effect is on what the store wants and charges.
  • Sickness exists only for players. At the start of development each player has a small chance of being sick, higher in dirty air, cured by holding 1 Medicine; a sick player walks at half pace. An epidemic makes every player use a Medicine or be sick. Colonists are never sick, and hunger and sickness are not linked anywhere.
  • The colony feeds back into production only through its mood. A restless or unhappy colony brings strikes (a good's plots make 20% unless the strikers are paid), walkouts (a player's main good makes 20%), MULE thefts, riots (the store loses a third of each good) and emigration. A dear Food price (over 1.5 times normal) makes strikes and riots likelier. The number of colonists sets the store's demand and prices.
  • Crews are 1 per raw plot and 2 per factory. They only add to their owner's Food and Water needs: they have no mood of their own.

How colonists feel when they go short

Three things decide it, each a number that can be tuned:

  1. A tolerance. Missing up to a tenth of what they need costs nothing.
  2. A curve. Past the tolerance the cost grows faster than the shortfall: a little short is a nudge, very short is a blow. The worse of Food and Water counts, because a colony with all its Water and no Food is starving.
  3. A memory. Each hungry month adds to a memory of hardship; each fed month halves it. The memory keeps the mood aiming lower and makes unrest likelier. The same shortfall month after month settles the memory at twice that month's share of hardship.

The mood still drifts at most 5 points a month toward where it is heading, as today. That keeps tax, air and prestige changes gentle. A hungry month's own blow lands at once, outside that limit, the way an event's does; otherwise any shortfall from 40% up would look the same.

Addendum: colony health

You asked for colonists who need Medicine, fall sick without it, pass sickness on to each other and to players and crews, a colony health gauge, and Electronics that make colonists happier but are never required. The colonists' side comes first; then what it asks of the store, the players and the crews; then what ten simulated games say.

What you'll see at the table

  • A health gauge beside the mood: the share of colonists who are well, from 100 down to 60. It never goes lower: at most 4 in 10 colonists are ever sick.
  • Hunger, thirst and missing Medicine make colonists sick, and sickness spreads. A few fall sick every month anyway; more when they went short; more again the more are already sick.
  • Fed and treated, they recover. About half the sick get well each month, fewer when Medicine is short. A colony that is fed and has its Medicine settles at about 3 sick in 100.
  • Sick colonists need more Medicine (up to 40% more), cost the mood up to 4 points of its aim, and slow the colony's growth by up to 2% a month.
  • Electronics are a comfort, never a need. When the store can give the colonists their Electronics (1 per 50 colonists a month), their mood aims up to 4 points higher. Missing them costs nothing.

How it works

Each month, after the colonists have eaten: new sick = the well share × (1 in 100 + up to 2 in 10 for hunger or thirst + up to 2 in 10 for missing Medicine + 15% of the share already sick). Recovered = half the sick share, cut by up to a third when Medicine is short. The sick share is capped at 4 in 10. Hunger and missing Medicine go through the same tolerance curve as the mood: a tenth short costs nothing.

Why it cannot spiral: contagion (15%) is far below recovery (50%), so whenever the colony is fed and treated, the sick share falls back toward 3 in 100 whatever it was. The ceiling only bounds the worst famine. The first version measured, with contagion equal to recovery, settled at 18 sick in 100 even when fully fed and sat at the ceiling to the end in 4 of 10 games; this one does not.

What it asks of the store, the players and the crews

  • Medicine. Colonists take their Medicine from the store at the month's end, free, after Food and Water: one a month for every 50 colonists, half as much again in dirty air, and up to 40% more while many are sick. It counts in what the colony needs, so a Medicine shortage raises its price through the same steps as Food.
  • Players. A player's chance of falling sick rises with the colony's sick share. Recommended as an addition (up to 10 points more at the worst), not a multiple: as a multiple, dirty air made players sick in 387 of 480 months.
  • Crews. Once a tenth of the colonists are sick, the same share of each player's crews is sick, and their plots make up to half as much on top of the hunger curve. One Medicine in the player's stock cures that player's crews for the month.
  • The gauge. Colony health shows beside the colony's mood as the share of colonists who are well. It is a new piece of screen, so its look needs a mock before it is built.
  • Electronics. Colonists want one a month for every 50 of them; when they get it the mood's aim rises, and when they don't, nothing is lost. It counts in what the colony needs, so its price climbs to four times normal (880) while nobody makes it. The ceiling stays at twelve times (2,640) as a safety rail only. At 880 the store pays 792 for a unit whose inputs (Rare earth, Alloy, Energy) cost about 175 at normal prices, or about 700 even if all three are short: a works pays well.

Decisions for you

1 · Bring in hardship together with the supply fix, or on its own first?

Today's simulated colony is short of Food or Water in 91 of 120 months, more than half short in 79. Any rule that counts how short they went makes that colony unhappier than today's flat 5 points does.

  • Together with the new consumption and demand, tuned in one pass. For: players first meet hardship in a colony that can be fed, so a hungry month means something. Against: hardship waits for the supply work.
  • On its own, now. For: the colony's real hunger shows at once. Against: most months would read Unhappy until supply catches up, and players would learn to ignore the mood.

Recommended: together.

2 · Where does the supply work start?

  • With power. Plants from the first month (the starting Energy, and the computer players' first plot), so that farms and wells run. For: the colony already has more farms than it needs if they are powered; power is what's missing. Against: none of our trial rules tested it yet; it is the supply pass's first job.
  • With more farms and wells. Against: the colony already has 14 farms where 6 would do.

Recommended: power first.

3 · How fast does Food spoil in a player's stock?

  • A quarter a month (today: half). For: the best colony of every rule we tried: fewest hungry months for players and colonists, 80 colonists at the end instead of 72. Against: stockpiling Food gets easier.
  • Half, as today. A player must grow twice what they eat to keep fed.

Recommended: a quarter. (Measured with farms and wells switched off last, which on its own changed nothing.)

4 · What does a hungry or thirsty crew do?

  • It works slower, on the curve above: nothing under a tenth short, down to half pace with nothing. The farm's “dry” rule goes. For: the worse the shortage the bigger the cost, never all-or-nothing, and the player whose crew it is sees why. Against: a new rule to show on the month's report.
  • It makes its player's walkout likelier (the colonists' team recommends this). For: the walkout already exists and players know it. Against: a walkout is a dice roll and all-or-nothing (the main good makes a fifth), so a slightly short crew and a starving one look the same until the dice fall.

Recommended: it works slower. Walkouts stay with the colony's mood, so the same hunger never counts twice. Measured: the colony makes 14.8 Food a month instead of 10.2, and colonists go short of Food in 61 months instead of 74.

5 · Should crews eat and drink more?

  • Not yet: keep the table, and raise it together with the power supply. For: on today's power, doubling what crews use cut the colony's Food from 14.8 to 4.8 a month and left 64 colonists instead of 74. Against: your sense that use is low is right (an uncut farm feeds the crews of 19 plots), and it waits.
  • Double it now. Against: measured above: the colony starves.

Recommended: not yet; set it in the same pass as the power supply.

6 · When power runs short, which plots switch off first?

  • As today: the biggest user first, then the least valuable.
  • Farms and wells last. Against: measured, it changed nothing: the power is short whichever plots wait.

Recommended: as today; more power is the fix.

7 · When is a player hungry?

  • When their household went short at the last month's end. For: it says what happened. Against: a player who bought Food late in the month still walks fed.
  • As today: when they start development holding less Food than they eat. Against: it reads the stock after half the Food has spoiled and before the month's market, so a player whose farms make twice their need still counts as hungry; 83 of every 100 player-months read hungry today.

Recommended: the household's last meal.

8 · Does hunger make a player sick more easily?

  • Not yet. The simulations never got the air dirty, so sickness is untested; decide it when it can be measured.
  • Yes: hunger raises the existing sickness odds by up to half. For: one more link in the chain. Against: untested; it stacks on the slower walk.

Recommended: not yet.

9 · What counts as the colony's stock?

  • The store's shelf plus what players hold. For: it is the overall supply you described; a colony whose players hold Food is not short. Against: a player hoarding Food keeps the price normal while others go short.
  • The store's shelf only. For: exactly what the store can see. Against: the store usually holds less than a month while players hold the rest, so prices would sit in the shortage bands most months.

Recommended: shelf plus players. We simulated both: counting only the shelf was worse on every count, including the colonists' Food.

10 · Steps or a smooth curve?

  • Four steps (the chart above). For: easy to say at the table: "Food is short". Against: the price jumps at a step's edge (38 to 60).
  • A smooth curve through the same points. For: no jumps. Against: no name for the state the colony is in.
  • A rise each month while it lasts (+15%, +40%, +80% a month by band). For: a long shortage keeps climbing. Against: the price depends on history, is hard to predict, and overshoots when supply returns.

Recommended: four steps.

11 · How fast does the price come down?

  • A quarter a month (120 to 30 in five months). For: a crisis is felt for a while, then forgotten. Against: Food stays dear a few months after the farms recover.
  • Half the gap a month (about four months).
  • Straight back to normal. For: simplest. Against: no memory of the crisis; the price snaps.

Recommended: a quarter a month. The chart above shows all three.

12 · Can a glut push the price below normal?
